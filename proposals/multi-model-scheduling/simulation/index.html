The result the simulation gives: within reasonable ranges, the system is essentially flat. Honest operators can tune any of these parameters within a wide band around the default and earnings barely change — the design is robust by construction. Where curves do show structure, it's at extreme values that obviously break things (e.g. switch_cooldown=30 ≈ 1 month, locks an operator into the wrong model for too long, dropping earnings ~3%).

This is the property we want. Operators who set defaults get good behavior. Operators who tweak don't hurt themselves unless they pick extreme values. The defaults are chosen as round numbers in the robust zone, not magic numbers.

What this is

An agent-based simulation of the multi-model scheduling design in GIP-XX. The chain provides demand and pricing signals; each operator's DAPI runs a local rational agent that decides which model to serve next epoch.

Each epoch, every operator:

  1. Reads current per-model supply and demand from the chain.
  2. Reads other operators' switching-intent announcements, weighted by their honesty reputation (a sigmoid of their past announcement fulfillment rate).
  3. Computes expected value per candidate model based on capacity-vs-demand pricing: EV(m) = (throughput/supply) × min(demand, supply) × basePrice × (1 + 4 × max(0, (demand-supply)/demand)). Under-served models pay more per inference, attracting operators until supply catches up to demand.
  4. Switches if a candidate beats the current model by at least switch_threshold + switch_cost_fraction.
  5. Announces the new plan (honestly, or with a small probability of bluffing).

The reputation system makes announcements meaningful without giving the chain a centralized scheduler. Operators who systematically lie lose signal weight; occasional bluffing is tolerated. The simulation finds a Nash equilibrium personal lying rate of ~10–20% — see the GIP's "Simulation Framework" section.

Model

  • 256 operators across 3 hardware tiers (small/mid/flagship at throughput 1×/4×/10×, 50%/35%/15% of the population).
  • 5 models with tier requirements and per-model base demand.
  • Per-model demand is mostly static (represents real user inference traffic; small noise + slow drift, no dramatic swings).
  • Pricing is capacity-vs-demand: when supply < demand the per-inference price ratchets up (1 + 4 × unmet_ratio, capped at 5×). This is what makes under-served models attractive and drives equilibrium.
  • Per epoch: 2 iterations of asynchronous cheap-talk decision-making (each iteration, 30% of operators randomly update their plan).
  • Honesty reputation uses a sigmoid discount with the GIP's specified canonical parameters.
  • "Why these defaults?" data is from 2000-epoch runs (500-epoch warmup discarded) with 3 random seeds per parameter value.

Limitations

This is a simulation, not a proof. It captures the announcement-and-reputation game but abstracts away: cross-DAPI variance in agent implementations, real switching dynamics (weights download time, container restart), validation interactions, BLS DKG participation, fee dynamics, real network heterogeneity. Treat it as evidence the defaults are sensible under reasonable assumptions, not as a guarantee.
